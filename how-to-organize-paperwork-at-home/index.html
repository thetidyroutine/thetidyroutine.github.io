How to Organize Paperwork at Home - A Simple Filing System

By Mr. Tidy · Organization · 2026-11-01

Paper piles up because every sheet asks you to make a decision, and it's easier to put it down "for now". The fix isn't a complicated filing cabinet. It's a system so simple that deciding takes seconds: one place where paper arrives, one place where it's kept, and a short weekly habit.

Here's a setup you can build in an afternoon.

Step 1: Gather every piece of paper

Collect it all in one spot: kitchen counter, junk drawer, bags, the top of the fridge, the car. Use a large box or a laundry basket. Don't sort yet. Seeing the whole pile once is what makes the next steps quick.

Then put on some music and go through it, one sheet at a time, into four piles:

  • Act: bills to pay, forms to return, anything with a deadline.
  • Keep: documents you may need later.
  • Scan or photo: things you want to be able to look up but don't need on paper.
  • Recycle or shred: everything else. Most of the pile usually ends up here.

Shred anything with personal or account details before throwing it out.

Step 2: Set up one inbox

Choose a single tray or basket near the door or wherever mail lands. From now on, every piece of paper that comes into the home goes here, and nowhere else. It's fine if it fills up during the week - it's a holding spot, not a filing system.

Step 3: Create a small set of hanging folders

For most households, a dozen broad categories are plenty. More folders means more decisions, and that's how filing systems get abandoned. Try these:

  1. Identity: copies of passports, IDs, birth and marriage certificates
  2. Home: lease or mortgage, utilities, repairs
  3. Insurance: home, car, health, life
  4. Medical: records, prescriptions, vaccination details
  5. Money: bank and investment statements
  6. Taxes: one sub-folder per year
  7. Car: registration, service records
  8. Work: contracts, pay statements
  9. School and kids: enrollment, reports, keepsakes
  10. Warranties and manuals: one folder, newest in front
  11. Pets: vet records (if needed)
  12. To file: a temporary spot for the weekly session

Label the tabs clearly. Hanging folders are great because they stay upright and slide easily, and you just drop the paper in without punching holes.

How long should you keep things?

Rules differ by country and situation, so check official guidance or ask your tax advisor for anything important. As a rough starting point in the US:

  • Keep long term: birth and marriage certificates, passports, property deeds, wills, and insurance policies that are still active.
  • Tax returns and supporting documents: the IRS generally recommends keeping them for at least three years, and longer in some situations.
  • Bank statements and utility bills: many people keep them for about a year, or use online access instead, unless they're needed for taxes.
  • Warranties and manuals: for as long as you own the item. Many manuals are also available online.

Originals of truly important documents, such as certificates, deserve a fireproof document box or a safe-deposit box rather than a regular folder.

What about keepsakes and kids' artwork?

Sentimental paper doesn't belong in the filing system, because you'll never make a quick decision about it. Give it a separate home: one box per person, with a limit you've agreed on in advance. When the box is full, choose what to take out before adding anything new.

For children's artwork, take a photo of each piece and keep only the ones you truly love. A photo book made from the rest once a year takes up far less space than a growing stack, and it's much more likely to be looked at.

Greeting cards, tickets and old letters can go in the same box. Keep it somewhere dry, and away from the basement if that tends to get damp.

Step 4: Go digital where it helps

Your phone's document scanning feature is good enough for most household paper. Save scans in cloud storage with folders that match your paper system, and use a simple naming pattern like 2026-11 electric bill, so that searching works later.

Switching bills and statements to paperless where you can stops the paper before it arrives. Keep a backup of anything important, and use a strong password on the account.

Step 5: Fifteen minutes once a week

The system only works with a short, regular session. Pick a fixed time, such as Sunday evening, and empty the inbox:

  1. Recycle or shred what isn't needed.
  2. Pay or act on what needs doing, or add it to your calendar.
  3. File what's left in the right folder.

That's it. If it's on your weekly cleaning schedule, it becomes a habit like any other job. A small whiteboard near your desk helps, too: anything with a deadline goes on the board, so you can see it without digging through paper.

Once a year: clear out the folders

Set a date to go through the folders, perhaps when you do your taxes. Remove anything out of date, start a new tax folder, and check that insurance documents are current. It takes less than an hour, and it keeps the system small enough to keep using.
