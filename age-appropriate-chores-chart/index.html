Age-Appropriate Chores - A Simple Chore Chart for Every Age

By Mr. Tidy · Cleaning Routines · 2026-11-01

This post contains affiliate links. If you buy through them, I may earn a small commission at no extra cost to you. Learn more.

A tidy home is much easier when it isn't one person's job. Children can usually do more than we expect, and the small jobs they learn now become habits later. The trick is matching the chore to the age, showing them how, and having a simple chart so nobody needs to keep asking.

Every child is different, so treat this list as a starting point rather than a rulebook, and always keep safety in mind with hot surfaces, sharp objects and cleaning products.

Ages 2 to 3: little helpers

At this age, "helping" is the whole point. It'll take longer than doing it yourself, and that's fine.

  • Put toys into a basket
  • Put dirty clothes in the hamper
  • Throw things in the trash
  • Wipe up small spills with a cloth
  • Help feed a pet, with you beside them

Low baskets and open bins make this far easier, because they can put things away without help. Our toy organization ideas have more on setting that up.

Ages 4 to 5: simple daily routines

Now they can follow a short routine, especially with pictures or a chart to look at.

  • Make their bed (it doesn't have to be perfect)
  • Tidy their room with a little help
  • Set the table with unbreakable items
  • Sort laundry into lights and darks
  • Match socks
  • Water plants

Ages 6 to 8: real responsibility

Kids this age can handle jobs on their own once you've shown them how a few times.

  • Get dressed and pack their school bag
  • Put their clean clothes away
  • Clear the table and load the dishwasher, with help for sharp or heavy items
  • Dust low surfaces
  • Sweep small areas
  • Feed pets and refill their water

Ages 9 to 12: running part of the home

Many children at this age can take full ownership of a few jobs.

  • Do a full load of laundry, with some guidance
  • Vacuum and mop
  • Clean the bathroom sink and mirror
  • Take out the trash and recycling
  • Help prepare simple meals
  • Tidy shared spaces after using them

Teenagers: nearly independent

By their teens, kids can do most household jobs. The goal now is preparing them to run their own home one day.

  • Clean the whole bathroom
  • Cook a simple family meal once a week
  • Do their own laundry from start to finish
  • Change their bed sheets
  • Plan a simple weekly budget for pocket money or a part-time job
  • Help with yard work or washing the car
  • Write the shopping list and help with the shop

How to set up a chore chart that lasts

The chart isn't the magic. The routine around it is. These steps make it stick:

  1. Start with two or three chores. It's better to do a few consistently than to hand out ten and give up after a week.
  2. Teach each job once, together. Do it with them first, then watch them do it, and then let them do it alone. Be specific: "clean your room" is vague, while "toys in the bin, clothes in the hamper" is clear.
  3. Fix a time. Tie chores to something that already happens, such as "before breakfast" or "after dinner". Our 15-minute evening reset works well as a family habit.
  4. Put the chart where everyone sees it. The fridge door or next to the kitchen table works best.
  5. Let them tick it themselves. Crossing it off is half the fun.
  6. Hold a quick family check-in once a week. Sunday works well: what went well, what should change, and who wants to swap a job?

Sharing chores between siblings

Fairness matters a lot to children, so make the rules visible. Rotating chores weekly means nobody is stuck with the job they dislike, and younger children can pair up with an older sibling for bigger tasks. Write names directly onto the chart, so there's no arguing over whose turn it is.

Should chores be paid?

Families do this differently, and there's no single right answer. One approach that many parents like is to separate the two: everyday jobs are simply part of being in the family and aren't paid, while bigger, extra jobs, like washing the car, can earn pocket money. Whatever you pick, explain it clearly and stay consistent. For small children, a sticker or a tick on the chart is usually reward enough.

When it stops working

Every chore chart gets ignored at some point. Don't take that as failure. Try these before giving up:

  • Change the job. Rotate chores every month, so they don't get boring.
  • Make it smaller. If a chore keeps being skipped, it might be too big. Break it down.
  • Do it at the same time. Working alongside them for ten minutes often beats reminding them five times.
  • Lower your standards a little. A crooked bed made by a six-year-old is a win. Don't redo it in front of them.

I'll admit that last one is hard for someone who lines up the pens on his desk. It's still worth it.
